The mechanism, alive

This is the machine turning: every gear with the real measurements read from the CT scan, driven by the same ratios as 2,100 years ago. Drag to turn around it; the controls are below.

The wheels move apart along the crank axis: in this model those of the calendar and the Moon sit on eleven different depths, from +6.5 mm for the big b1 down to −12.5 mm for the last plane; in front of b1, up to about 36 mm, sit those of the planets. Turn the labels on and look for k1 and k2: two 50-tooth wheels on arbors that are not aligned, one carrying a pin that runs in the other’s slot — that is how the machine makes the Moon run faster near perigee and slower near apogee. The pale green wheels are the hypothetical ones: they are not in the artefact.

The sky above Siracusa

The mechanism knows nothing of place: it computes longitudes on the ecliptic, valid anywhere. The place is needed to translate them into the sky one sees: rising and setting with azimuths, altitude above the horizon at the set hour, and whether the predicted eclipse is visible from there. Times in local mean time (UT + longitude/15), the same for every epoch.

sky dome seen from above: N at top, zenith at centre; the marks on the rim are the azimuths of rising and setting

What the machine is telling you

Front dial — zodiac ring & Egyptian calendar

The dial people looked at: the two hands are the Sun (gold) and the Moon (silver) on the zodiac; the outer ring is the 365-day Egyptian calendar. The small disc below shows the lunar phase.

Pointer readings

The same hands, read as numbers. “Anomaly” is the correction the pin-and-slot applies to the Moon, because it does not move at constant speed.

Metonic spiral — 235 months / 5 turns

19 years make exactly 235 lunar months: this spiral tells which month of the cycle we are in, and what it was called.

Saros spiral — 223 months / 4 turns

223 lunar months: after one Saros the eclipses repeat. The hand points to the cell of the cycle; the original machine had the eclipse months inscribed on it.

Subsidiary dials

Three small dials: the year of the Panhellenic Games (Olympia, Nemea…), the 76-year Callippic cycle, and the Exeligmos that shifts eclipse times by 8 hours per Saros.

Where the data come from

Everything this page computes starts from published measurements, not estimates. The 30 surviving gears — tooth counts, radii, thicknesses — come from the CT scan of the artefact kept at the National Archaeological Museum in Athens: counts in Freeth et al., Nature 2006; measurements in millimetres in Table S8 of Freeth et al., Scientific Reports 2021. The back dials — Metonic calendar, Games, Saros, Exeligmos — follow Freeth, Jones, Steele & Bitsakis, Nature 2008. The calibration epoch (28 April 205 BC, the start of the Saros dial) is the hypothesis of Freeth, PLOS ONE 2014, and remains a hypothesis. The planets follow the Cosmos model of Freeth et al. 2021 (period relations and Table S9); the tooth counts for Mars, Jupiter and Saturn are not published in readable form and are derived here, and marked as such.

The comparison with the sky uses Meeus’s formulae (Astronomical Algorithms) for Sun and Moon and JPL Keplerian elements for the planets: accuracy of a few arc-minutes. The design choices — the 3D layout, the layer depths, the eccentricity of the solar pin, the patina — are listed in the Expert view.

The Cosmos — the five “little spheres”

The Cosmos — the five “little spheres”

No planetary gearing survives: this part is reconstruction, not artefact. The period relations are those of Freeth et al. 2021; the Mercury and Venus tooth counts are printed in that text, while those for Mars, Jupiter and Saturn are derived here to satisfy the published relations and the shared-gear constraints. Table S9 of the paper confirms the design: the ratios between pin distance and axis distance (14.04/36.0 = 0.39 · 20.01/27.8 = 0.72 · 10.00/6.58 = 1.52 · 8.22/1.58 = 5.20 · 14.37/1.50 = 9.58) reproduce the planetary distances in astronomical units. The model is therefore one of concentric circles: synodic anomaly yes, zodiacal anomaly no.

The mechanism against the sky

The mechanism against the sky

The machine computes with fixed whole-number ratios. The sky does not. The deviation below is the object's real error, and it depends on how far you are from the calibration: the pointers are set by hand, and the operator would reset them. Calibrated to the ancient epoch, the Moon is more than 200° out at today's date and the Sun 14 days out — the effect of 22 centuries of cranking. Calibrated to the date entered, the machine is accurate again: that is how it would have been used.

TO BE VERIFIED — the comparison formulae (Meeus, truncated series: Sun 3 terms, Moon ~20 terms in longitude and ~13 in latitude) are polynomials fitted to the modern epoch. Towards −200 the uncertainty grows to a few arcminutes, compounded by the uncertainty in ΔT (Earth rotation), not modelled here: dates are treated as Terrestrial Time. Two different reference frames are also mixed: Sun and Moon are referred to the equinox of date (Meeus), the planets to the J2000 equinox (JPL Keplerian elements, the 3000 BC–3000 AD table with the additional b, c, s, f terms for Jupiter and Saturn). Precession opens a gap between the two frames of roughly 1.4° per century from 2000. Publishable work needs a real ephemeris (DE440 / Skyfield).

Scope of the model

Scope of the model

  • The zodiacal anomaly of the planets. The Cosmos model is one of concentric circles: it reproduces the synodic anomaly (retrogradations included) but not orbital eccentricity. For Mars, the most eccentric of the five, the departure from the ephemeris can exceed 10°. It is the limit of the reconstructed model.
  • The eclipse glyphs. On the Saros spiral the eclipses are not computed by the gearing: they are inscribed, cell by cell. The eclipse indicator here is a modern astronomical calculation (lunar latitude at syzygy), not an epigraphic reading: it shows where the inscription should have carried a glyph.
  • The 365-hole ring. Budiselić et al. 2024 and Woan & Bayley 2024 argue, from the spacing of the surviving holes, that the calendar ring carried 354 holes (a lunar year) rather than 365. The question is open; this model adopts 365, following the 2006/2021 reconstruction.
  • The Dragon Hand. The lunar node pointer is entirely hypothetical (train 49~62+64~48, −5/93 turns per year, 18.6 years). Not implemented here.
